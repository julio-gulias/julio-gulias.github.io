Hiring Has a Confidence Problem, Not Just an Application-Volume Problem.

How AI-driven resume tailoring is changing recruiter confidence — and why career records, ATS platforms, and portable trust may need to work together.

AI Hiring Recruiting Talent Acquisition HR Tech Job Search Career Strategy

Originally published on LinkedIn on September 29, 2026

AI has made tailored resumes almost infinitely regenerable. That is good for legitimate tailoring — but it changes what a resume can tell a recruiter.

When hundreds of candidates can produce highly aligned resumes at very low cost, the challenge is no longer only identifying who appears to fit the job. It is also deciding how much confidence to place in the professional representation behind that fit.

Since becoming a job hunter in March this year, I have acquired an unintended daily job: applying for jobs.

And one thing becomes obvious very quickly. Many positions receive hundreds of applications within just a few hours of being posted.

From an economic perspective, that may not be an encouraging sign. It may mean that many qualified professionals are competing for a relatively smaller number of opportunities.

But from a recruiter's perspective, we might expect exactly the opposite. An abundant supply of strong professionals should make hiring easier, not harder.

Let's imagine a simple scenario.

A position receives 500 applications. Most of the candidates are genuinely qualified, they submit strong resumes, and the ATS can identify with reasonable confidence those whose experience is most closely aligned with the job description.

Even if many candidates end up very close to one another, the recruiter does not necessarily need to find the single "perfect" person.

If the final group is made up of genuinely strong candidates, even an imperfect selection will probably still result in a good hire.

This is where artificial intelligence changes the equation.

The cost of producing a resume that is highly aligned with a job description has fallen dramatically. AI tools can identify keywords, reorganize experience, prioritize relevant accomplishments, improve the writing, and adapt the same professional history to different opportunities in a matter of minutes.

That, by itself, is not a problem.

Tailoring is legitimate. Candidates can — and should — present their experience differently depending on the opportunity.

Automation is not the problem either, as long as the resulting representation remains grounded in the professional's actual career.

But lower production cost has another important consequence:

resumes have become highly dynamic.

A professional may have one version for a Program Manager role, another for a Customer Success position, another for Professional Services leadership, and several more tailored to different opportunities — all created within the same week, sometimes on the same day.

There is nothing inherently wrong with that.

In fact, this is exactly what good tailoring is supposed to do: surface different parts of the same career depending on the context.

But a resume is also a largely private, application-specific document. It may be seen by only one employer, exist for one hiring process, and never be used again.

That makes it fundamentally different from something like a LinkedIn profile.

A LinkedIn profile is a shared, relatively persistent professional representation, expected to remain reasonably stable across many simultaneous interactions. It cannot realistically be rewritten for every application without losing that persistence.

A resume has no such constraint.

AI has therefore not simply made resumes easier to write.

It has made them almost infinitely regenerable.
One relatively stable underlying career can now generate dozens of highly optimized professional representations at very low cost.

Or, put another way:

The career is relatively stable. Its representations are becoming increasingly dynamic.

The challenge is not to prevent that dynamism.

The challenge is to keep those representations grounded in the same underlying career.

And that is where optimization can cross a line that is surprisingly subtle.

AI can strengthen the meaning of a statement even when the candidate never intended to exaggerate. Someone who supported an initiative may become someone who managed it. Someone who managed something may suddenly have led it. A contribution to an outcome may, after several rounds of optimization, begin to look like ownership of that outcome.

Two individually true facts can also be combined in a way that creates an inaccurate interpretation.

And, of course, the same technology can be deliberately used to exaggerate or fabricate experience.

A recruiter does not need to know whether a particular resume was written with AI for the problem to exist.

It is enough to know that it is now extremely easy to produce highly polished, strongly aligned applications — some completely defensible, some semantically inflated, and some potentially containing information that would not withstand deeper scrutiny.

So let's return to those 500 candidates.

The ATS can still identify who appears to have the strongest fit.

But now a second question emerges — one that the ranking itself does not answer:

How much confidence can I place in whether that apparent fit accurately represents the career behind the resume?

This is not about proving whether a candidate is truthful or dishonest.

It is about having enough confidence in the professional representation to decide which candidates deserve the next, more costly stage of human review.

That is where the problem changes.

Application volume still matters, but perhaps volume is more of an amplifier than the root cause.

The deeper bottleneck may be confidence.

When many candidates look excellent on paper, but the recruiter has limited information to distinguish a well-optimized representation from a well-grounded one, abundance stops being purely an advantage.

It also creates uncertainty.

Volume amplifies the problem. Uncertainty about the reliability of the representation is what can turn it into a confidence crisis.

A career is bigger than a resume

Thinking about this led me to look at the way I build my own applications.

Long before AI became part of that process, I already had a habit that eventually became extremely useful.

I do not easily remember precise details from events that happened many years ago. So whenever I left a company, I tried to document what I had done there: projects, responsibilities, decisions, results, problems I had faced, and other details that might otherwise fade from memory over time.

With the arrival of AI, those notes became incredibly valuable.

Today, they form the foundation from which I build my applications.

Instead of starting with my previous resume and simply adapting it to a new opportunity, I start with a much broader view of my career and select from it what is relevant to that particular role.

My career is much bigger than any one of my resumes — and much bigger than what appears on my LinkedIn profile.

A resume and a LinkedIn profile are representations of a career, created for different purposes.

Neither of them is the career itself.

Even when working from that broader reference, however, I noticed that AI can introduce small distortions during tailoring.

A contribution can acquire a sense of ownership that was never there. A responsibility can appear larger than it really was. Two facts that are correct independently can create a misleading claim when compressed into the same sentence.

That is why I use additional checks to compare the resulting resume against the professional history it came from and correct any unintended inflation.

My purpose here is not to explain how I tailor resumes.

What caught my attention was the principle behind the process.

I can evaluate a specific representation of my career more confidently because I can compare it with a much broader and more persistent reference of what I actually did.

That led me to a question:

If this principle works for an individual professional, could something similar work for the broader labor market?

If a resume is only one specific representation of a career, why do we almost always evaluate that representation in isolation?

What if there were a broader, longitudinal reference — controlled by the professional, not by the employer — against which that representation could be assessed?

Think of it as a persistent, structured record of roles, responsibilities, achievements, evidence, and context — something deliberately broader than any single resume.

The goal would not be to make every resume look the same.

Quite the opposite.

Tailoring would remain both legitimate and desirable.

The idea would simply be to allow different representations to remain different while still staying grounded in the same underlying career.

From personal experience to a market problem

With that principle in mind, I began developing a framework to assess consistency between a professional representation — such as a resume — and the broader career behind it.

The objective was easy to describe, even if much harder to implement: reduce the uncertainty faced by the people who have to select a handful of candidates from hundreds of applications.

The exercise became intellectually rewarding because it forced me to look at the problem at a much broader level.

What initially looked like a question about resumes and AI use began to look more like a confidence problem distributed across the hiring ecosystem.

So I started researching what different players in the market are already doing about it.

One of the clearest pressure points is recruiting teams.

They are the ones who have to turn hundreds — sometimes thousands — of applications into a shortlist small enough to justify deeper human review.

The data helps put that pressure into perspective.

Greenhouse's 2026 benchmark analyzed more than 6,000 companies and more than 640 million applications between 2022 and 2025. Over that period, applications per job increased by 111%, while the average number of recruiters per organization fell by 56%. Time-to-fill increased by 37%. At the same time, monthly hires per recruiter increased by 122%.

That suggests something more nuanced than simply saying recruiting has become less efficient.

Recruiters are processing much more volume, with smaller teams and higher individual productivity — yet within a process that is still taking longer overall.

A recent BBC report also illustrates how strongly AI has entered the candidate side of the process.

The report cited a survey in which 73% of students and recent graduates had used AI at some point during the application process, while 21% of recruiters surveyed reported using AI specifically for shortlisting.

Those populations and use cases are different, so the figures should not be compared directly, but they do illustrate the speed of the transformation.

There are also more direct signs of friction.

In a Canadian Robert Half survey published in March 2026, 61% of HR leaders said reviewing AI-generated applications was slowing hiring, 89% reported heavier workloads, and 64% of hiring managers said AI-enhanced resumes were making skills harder to verify.

So perhaps the issue is not that recruiters are doing less.

It may be exactly the opposite.

They are doing more with less, while an increasing share of that effort must be spent not only identifying fit, but establishing enough confidence to decide who deserves to move forward.

The market is already responding

Applicant Tracking System providers are naturally responding to this environment.

Greenhouse, for example, developed Real Talent, combining AI-powered candidate matching, fraud detection, and identity verification inside the hiring workflow.

Its fraud-detection layer analyzes signals such as phone number, email address, IP address, and location. Candidate matching prioritizes applications against recruiter-defined criteria, while identity verification can confirm that the person progressing through the process is the applicant they claim to be. Greenhouse describes the product as a protection layer within the hiring workflow.

That is a perfectly rational response for an ATS: use the data, signals, and integrations available within the boundaries of the product to increase employer confidence.

But there is a structural limitation.

By design, an ATS has a strong view of the application and hiring workflow.

It typically has a much more limited view of the professional's longitudinal career.

It may analyze application metadata, verify identity, compare previous applications when they exist, and consult external sources when available.

But none of those sources, by itself, necessarily represents the candidate's broader career.

Coverage also varies.

One candidate may have previous applications with that employer; another may never have applied there before.

One professional may have easily verifiable credentials and an extensive public presence. Another may have an equally legitimate career that happens to be much less visible publicly.

That makes it difficult to build uniform confidence using only what each ATS can observe.

Until this point, these were the players I knew best: employers, recruiters, ATS platforms, job boards, and professional networks such as LinkedIn.

The framework I had been developing, however, assumed the existence of another type of participant: an entity capable of maintaining that broader, more persistent, longitudinal career reference.

For the purposes of the framework, I started calling them Career Record Providers — CRPs.

This is not an established market category. It is simply the label I use to describe that function.

When I went looking for them, I discovered that many of the pieces already exist.

Europass, for example, allows professionals to maintain a broad profile containing skills, education, work experience, achievements, and other information, then use all or part of that information to create different CVs for particular opportunities.

In January 2026, the HR Open Standards Consortium launched the Trusted Career Profile — TCP, designed to make career information portable and interoperable across Learning and Employment Records, verifiable credentials, ATS platforms, and talent-management systems. TCP combines LERs, verifiable credentials, and self-attested information that can include skills, employment history, narratives, and other career information.

There are also products and initiatives exploring career records, career passports, evidence, credentials, profile persistence, and job-search assets.

That research led to an important realization:

I was definitely not looking at an entirely new idea.

Many of the pieces I had imagined already exist — some in quite sophisticated forms.

The pieces exist. But where is the user pull?

I then tried some of these platforms myself as a job hunter.

That brought another lesson.

I do not wake up wanting to fill in a Career Record.

I want to find a job.

Job boards and talent platforms are naturally attractive because they begin with the professional's immediate need: opportunities.

Profiles, resumes, and other information come afterward as a necessary consequence.

Some of the Career Record platforms I tried reverse that sequence. They first ask the professional to invest time in structuring a career in exchange for a future benefit that can feel relatively abstract.

Even for me — someone deeply interested in this subject — that was not particularly attractive.

That observation certainly does not establish why these platforms have or have not achieved broader adoption.

But it did make me question the acquisition model.

The Career Record may not need to be the thing that initially brings the professional to a platform.

It may instead become the asset that gets built while the professional is using something they actually want to use.

Jobs can be the acquisition engine.

The Career Record can become the accumulated asset.

That creates an interesting position for job boards and talent platforms that already have candidates, vacancies, and recurring traffic.

On the other side, ATS vendors have something those candidate-side platforms usually do not: direct relationships with employers and presence exactly at the moment when hiring decisions need to be made.

Maybe the problem is coordination

At that point, my question changed again.

In my research so far, I have not found a broadly adopted approach that connects these pieces in a coordinated way specifically to address confidence at the top of the hiring funnel.

On one side, ATS vendors are developing their own ways of building confidence from the signals available to them.

On the other, Career Record initiatives are creating broader and more persistent career references, usually oriented toward the professional.

Job boards and talent platforms have candidates, jobs, and traffic.

Credential infrastructures can verify specific facts.

LinkedIn provides a persistent professional identity and a massive network, but its profile is still another representation of the career — shared, relatively stable, and necessarily incomplete.

And in the middle of this ecosystem sits the recruiter, reconciling fragmented signals and deciding which few candidates deserve to move forward.

Perhaps, then, the problem does not require one more isolated solution.

Perhaps it requires coordination between solutions that already exist.

Imagine a Career Record controlled by the professional and functioning as a longitudinal reference.

With the candidate's authorization, an ATS could use that reference to assess the representation submitted with a particular application.

Importantly, that does not mean giving an employer unrestricted access to someone's entire private career history.

The ATS would not necessarily need the full record. With candidate authorization, it could receive only the specific consistency or confidence signals needed for that hiring decision.

That would add a second dimension to Job Fit:

confidence in the representation

The ATS could continue answering:

Who appears to fit this job?

Another layer could help answer:

How much confidence can we place in the professional representation that produced that fit?

There would be no need to make every resume identical.

There would be no need to expose the professional's entire private career record.

There would be no need to replace ATS platforms, job boards, LinkedIn, or credential providers.

Each participant could continue doing what it already does best.

The Career Record would maintain a broader and more persistent reference.

The ATS would continue evaluating fit and managing the hiring workflow.

Job platforms would continue attracting professionals and opportunities.

Credential providers would continue strengthening the evidence behind specific facts.

And a consistency layer could help answer a question that remains expensive today:

How much confidence can we place in whether this specific representation is truly grounded in the career of the professional who submitted it?

Such a model could also reduce the need for every ATS vendor to reconstruct confidence independently.

Today, each vendor can integrate different sources, develop its own fraud indicators, retain historical applications, and establish its own approach to authenticity.

All of that can be rational individually.

But at the ecosystem level, it may also produce duplicated engineering, uneven candidate coverage, and different proprietary interpretations of the same underlying problem.

Perhaps the scarce resource is no longer candidate data.

The market already has enormous amounts of it.

Perhaps the scarce resource is:

coordinated, candidate-authorized, and portable confidence.

A possible opportunity between players that already exist

The more I researched the problem, the less it seemed to depend on creating a new tool that replaces what already exists.

Many of the pieces are already in the market.

Job boards and talent platforms know how to attract professionals because they offer what those professionals actually want: opportunities.

ATS platforms know the job, the application, and the hiring workflow.

Career Record initiatives are creating more persistent and structured professional references.

Identity, credentials, and verification are advancing quickly.

What still appears fragmented is the connection between these pieces.

That may create interesting opportunities for more agile players.

A talent platform does not need to become an ATS.

An ATS does not need to reconstruct the entire career of every candidate by itself.

And a Career Record platform may not need to convince professionals to fill in long forms simply for the abstract benefit of having a better career record.

A platform that already has the attention of job hunters could help them gradually build a persistent professional reference through normal use.

An ATS could, with the candidate's authorization, consume a signal from that reference and add confidence to Job Fit.

Each participant would continue doing what it already does best — but the combination could create something that none of them can deliver independently.

I do not know whether this is the right architecture.

I also do not know whether the market would call this Career Record, Career Consistency, representation confidence, or something else entirely.

Finding out where this hypothesis is wrong is actually one of the reasons I am writing about it.

What increasingly seems clear to me is this:

The hiring market already has enormous amounts of candidate data, but it is still learning how to turn that data into portable, consistent, and usable confidence early in the hiring process.

This is where I would especially like to hear from recruiters and Talent Acquisition professionals.

Where does confidence actually break down for you today — before the shortlist, during screening, or later in the process? And what kind of signal would materially increase that confidence without adding more work or friction?

I would also welcome perspectives from people building ATS, recruiting, job-marketplace, career-data, credential, and HR-tech products.

Perhaps the opportunity is not to reinvent any of these categories, but to discover how to make them work better together.

I am continuing to explore this problem and the framework that emerged from it.

I am not looking only for agreement. If you believe this hypothesis is wrong, incomplete, or already being solved in a way I have missed, I would genuinely like to hear from you.
