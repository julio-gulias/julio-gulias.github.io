From Assembly to English.

A personal reflection on the long climb from Assembly and C to AI, natural language, and the possibility that specifications may become executable.

AI Programming Software Development Abstraction Future of Work

Originally published on LinkedIn on October 3, 2026

A few days ago, I heard David Heinemeier Hansson — DHH, the creator of Ruby on Rails — say something that certainly helped stir up the famously calm and measured software development community.

He said he had not written code himself for months.

He was programming with artificial intelligence.

Then he went a little further: in his view, English was a better programming language than Ruby.

I imagine that last sentence inspired a number of perfectly reasonable and proportionate reactions on the internet.

Oddly enough, instead of making me think immediately about artificial intelligence, agents, or the future of programming as a profession, it took me back a very long way — to when I used to write firmware in Assembly for an 8051 microcontroller.

I would rather not say exactly what year that was.

I am currently looking for a job, and I see no reason to make ageism any easier.

Let's just say it was a while ago.

I had recently graduated in Electronic Engineering and was working on hardware development for hard disk drives. Among other things, we worked on positioning the mechanical system and processing the data being written to and read from the physical media.

I had already learned the fundamentals of programming at university, but that job was where I really went deep into it.

Programming an 8051 in Assembly gave you a rather intimate relationship with the machine.

You knew which registers you were using. You knew where the data was. You knew how many bytes you still had available.

And you knew when you were running out.

Memory, back then, was not the abstract resource we can afford to be relatively casual about today.

Every byte had a certain emotional value.

Processing speed did too.

One extra instruction was not necessarily just one extra instruction.

And yes, I used to do hexadecimal arithmetic in my head.

I am not particularly proud of that.

I also do not recommend it as a hobby.

At some point, I started using a tool that allowed me to program that same microcontroller in C. I no longer remember which one it was. At the time, C was considered a high-level language.

That sentence may require a few seconds to sink in for younger readers.

C. High level.

For me, it was a small revolution.

Suddenly, I could express what I wanted the machine to do in a way that was much closer to the logic of the problem and much less tied to the architecture of the microcontroller.

The work became easier.
I became more productive.
But there was a price.

The code produced automatically was not always as lean as what I would have written directly in Assembly. Libraries could bring along things I did not need. Some operations could be implemented more efficiently by hand.

And when memory and processing power are scarce, that bothers you.

I would look at certain pieces of generated code and think something along the lines of:

I could do this better.

And I probably could.

It was possible to spend hours squeezing out a few bytes here, saving a few cycles there, finding a more direct way to do something.

But another question was beginning to appear behind all of that:

Better for what?

As hardware improved, memory became cheaper, processors became faster, and those small inefficiencies began to matter less.

Human productivity, meanwhile, continued to matter a great deal.

At some point, it stopped making sense for me to spend a significant amount of time doing work the tool could do well enough for me.

That may have been my first direct experience with something that would happen again and again throughout the history of programming.

We keep moving up a level.

And every time we do, something gets hidden underneath.

Assembly moved us away from zeros and ones.

C moved us away from registers and many of the machine's individual instructions.

Then came other languages, frameworks, libraries, virtual machines, garbage collectors, ORMs, APIs, cloud services, and an apparently endless collection of layers that began doing automatically what some programmer, in an earlier era, would have controlled manually.

There is always a price. Sometimes we use more memory. Sometimes we use more processing power. Sometimes we lose control. Sometimes we do not even know exactly what is happening underneath.

And almost invariably, someone appears to explain that the previous generation used to build real software.

It is something of an industry tradition.

Perhaps every generation of programmers earns the right to complain about the abstractions created by the generation that follows.

I probably used up my share when I abandoned Assembly for C.

Decades later, listening to DHH say that English might be a better programming language than Ruby gave me a strangely familiar feeling.

Maybe we are simply moving up one more level.

Except this level is different.

Until now, even the languages we considered extremely high-level still required humans to learn a special way of talking to computers.

Syntax.
Types.
Structures.
Rules.
Conventions.

Now we are beginning to experiment with the opposite direction.

Perhaps computers are learning to work with the way we already communicate.

That does not mean English is really a programming language in the traditional sense.

In fact, English has a few characteristics that would make any compiler consider a career change.

It is ambiguous. It depends on context. It tolerates contradictions.

It allows sentences such as "make the interface simple and intuitive," which sound perfectly reasonable until someone has to define exactly what "simple" and "intuitive" mean.

But perhaps that is precisely what makes this interesting.

If I tell an AI:

"Build a system to register customers,"

I still have not described very much.

Who is allowed to create a customer? Which fields are required? Can there be duplicates? Who can edit a record? Who can delete one? What does "delete" actually mean? Do we need to keep history? Does the system need to work with ten customers or ten million?

The sentence may be perfectly good English and still be a rather poor program.

Or, more accurately, a poor specification.

And that is what made me wonder whether the most interesting transformation here is not really "programming in English."

Maybe it is something else.

Maybe we are beginning to make specifications executable.

I spent many years working on software projects after I stopped programming professionally.

And one thing I saw over and over again was documentation trying to keep up with the software.

Sometimes it managed. Often it did not.

The project moved. Decisions were made. Requirements changed. Bugs were fixed. Shortcuts were taken to reach an important date.

And somewhere, there was a document watching all of this happen from a distance.

Documentation had a well-known tendency to arrive later.

When it arrived at all.

That was not always negligence.

Often, it was simply economics.

If working software is what delivers value and time is limited, something eventually gets pushed aside.

It is not very difficult to guess what.

But what if that relationship reverses?

What if the things we now call requirements, business rules, expected behaviour, acceptance criteria, constraints, examples, exceptions, and architectural decisions become the very material used to produce the software?

In that case, writing a good specification would no longer mean preparing documentation before programming.

It would be programming.

Traditional code could become an intermediate layer.

Necessary, certainly. Important. But not necessarily the place where humans directly express the full intent of the system.

Perhaps someone writes:

"Customers cannot have two active records with the same identifier." "Customer-service users may update customer information but may not delete customers." "Every change must be auditable." "A search must respond in under half a second against a database of one million records."

And a machine turns those intentions into databases, APIs, tests, screens, validations, and whatever else is required.

I have no idea whether it will happen exactly like that.

And I am old enough — although we are still avoiding specific numbers — to be suspicious of anyone who knows with great certainty what the future of technology will look like.

I have seen too many predictions.

Some brilliant.
Some hilarious.
Sometimes the same ones.

I also do not imagine that deep technical knowledge will suddenly become less valuable.

When I started using C, understanding Assembly was still a huge advantage.

I knew what existed beneath the abstraction. I knew when I could trust it. And I knew when it might be better to drop down a level and solve something directly.

I suspect something similar will continue to happen.

Someone who understands algorithms, architecture, databases, security, networking, concurrency, and software engineering will probably be much better equipped to recognise when something produced by an AI makes sense — and when it does not.

But that is different from having to personally type every line that turns human intent into machine behaviour.

Maybe that is the part of this story I find most amusing.

I spent the beginning of my career learning to speak more and more fluently with computers.

First, almost in their language. Then in a language a little closer to mine.

Later, I stopped programming professionally and spent much of my time trying to get people to explain, clearly enough, what they wanted computers to do.

Requirements.
Scope.
Behaviours.
Acceptance criteria.
Priorities.
Exceptions.
Context.

For a long time, all of that seemed to exist around the code.

Now I find myself wondering whether, at some point, it may exist above it.

Perhaps the documentation that spent decades running behind the software, desperately trying to catch up, will end up doing something unexpected.

Perhaps it will move ahead.

Perhaps it will even become the code.

And if that really happens, there is a particularly pleasant irony in it for someone who once did hexadecimal arithmetic in his head: after spending decades learning to speak like computers, perhaps we are finally teaching computers to understand the way we speak.
